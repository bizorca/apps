## Origin and hard times

**The van down by the river**
The northern Colorado real estate market crashed, he stopped paying his bills, took a $9-an-hour cell phone activation job at a call center, filed Chapter 7 the day after his house was foreclosed, and lived in a van through one of the coldest Colorado winters. He frames it with the Chris Farley SNL sketch, shows a photo of the van (a bed, a dresser he built, storage bins, a pistol safe), and adds that he also owned a '96 Saturn ("I had two cars for a homeless guy, I was a baller"). Once he had the tax job he parked in the firm's lot and slept on its office couch when it got cold. In the 2016 Orlando telling he says there were nights the pistol was against his own head; that detail appears in no other telling and is his call to use.
Use it for: credibility from rock bottom; if he started from nothing at $10 an hour, the reader has no excuse.
Told in: Leadership webinar (c. 2013), TPS Miami (2014), Strategies (2015), Blueprint (2015), Discomfort Atlanta (2015), 1120S (2015), Orlando (2016), Marketing Plan Pt 4 (2017), Marketing 101 (c. 2017), Quoting Fees (2017), 12-Step live (2018), Getting to One (2018), Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018), Outsourcing (2018), B2B Workshop (c. 2018-19), Top Strategies (2020); 16 tellings, plus Dan Henn's secondhand version in Lien Workshop 7 (c. 2019).
Quote: "it's a great sketch, it's incredibly funny, but it's not so funny when you're actually living in a van down by Boulder Creek."
Check:
- Time in the van: about 10 months (Strategies 2015; Bankrupt-to-$1M interview 2018); about a year (Marketing 101); 8 months (TPS Miami 2014); 8 months in one and 10 in the other of Discomfort Atlanta (2015) and Getting to One (2018).
- Where: by Boulder Creek (Strategies 2015, one of two tellings in that talk; TPS Miami 2014); unnamed river elsewhere.
- Bankruptcy timeline: stopped paying bills in early or February 2007, stopped the mortgage September 2007, filed the day after foreclosure (Blueprint 2015; TPS Miami 2014); Chapter 7 "at the end of 2007" (Strategies 2015) or "by late 2007" (Marketing 101); Washington Mutual foreclosed April 30, 2008 and he filed the next day (Bankrupt-to-$1M interview 2018); stopped paying in February 2007 but filed April 2008 (Discomfort Atlanta 2015); foreclosure "early 2008" (Orlando 2016).
- Divorce: "a couple of years" before (Strategies 2015; Orlando 2016); "about 2005" (12-Step live 2018); November 2005 (Bankrupt-to-$1M interview 2018).

**Jackson Hewitt in four days**
Broke and driving around, he saw a "free tax class" sign at a Jackson Hewitt, guessed they hired preparers out of it, finished the class in days, fixed the franchise's crashed network, and was hired on the spot. He worked the 2008 season at a kiosk inside the Super Walmart on Mulberry in Fort Collins, a step up from the call center.
Use it for: demonstrated expertise gets you hired; small steps up from the bottom ($9, then $9.50, then $10).
Told in: TPS Miami (2014), Strategies (2015), Blueprint (2015), Discomfort Atlanta (2015), 1120S (2015), Orlando (2016), Marketing 101 (c. 2017), Getting to One (2018), Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018), Top Strategies (2020); 10 tellings.
Quote: "So after that little demonstration of expertise, they hired me on the spot and there's a lesson to be learned there."
Check:
- Course length and his time: weeks-long, done in four days (Strategies 2015); four weeks, done in two and a half days, network fixed the third afternoon (Blueprint 2015); four to five weeks, done in four days, network fixed in under two hours on day five (TPS Miami 2014); six to eight weeks, done in about four days, network fixed on exam day (Discomfort Atlanta 2015).
- Pay: $9.50 (Blueprint 2015; TPS Miami 2014; one of Discomfort Atlanta 2015 / Getting to One 2018); $8.50 (the other of those two); $9.10 (Orlando 2016 or Leadership webinar c. 2013, per the notes).
- When: "very end of 2007" (Strategies 2015); November 2007 (Discomfort Atlanta 2015).

**The Social Security statement**
He pulled his earnings record and read it back to 1993, when as a high-school sophomore he swept floors and touched up paint on new houses for $5.75 an hour. In 20 years he never made more than about $40,000, with years of $3,000, zero, $9,000, and about $2,000.
Use it for: proof of a genuinely low-income starting point and no retirement history.
Told in: Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018); 1 telling.
Quote: "My first job was building houses In the neighborhood that I lived in And I was on just Sweeping floors"

**Four and a half years in vehicles**
Raised by a single mother who moved state to state for work, he lived in the car at times as a child. In the Navy, a pay error stopped his checks and he lived in his car for six months in Charleston during nuclear prototype training, and after the crash he lived in the van. By 40 he totals about four to four and a half years, and still thinks of the car as an acceptable fallback.
Use it for: poverty mentality, and the low "default" he has had to raise.
Told in: Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018); 1 telling.
Quote: "I spent six months living in my car in Charleston at prototype."

**The investor club at the library**
Newly divorced in November 2005 and living in his own downtown Fort Collins condo after a tenant left, he wandered into the library across the street and found a standing-room-only real estate investor meeting about buying "subject to" existing loans. For three or four years he was the running gag who asked every week how to start with no money, after the answer had been given.
Use it for: something can make intellectual sense without making emotional sense; money beliefs lag knowledge.
Told in: Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018); 1 telling.
Quote: "it just made intellectual sense, but it didn't make emotional sense, I guess."

**The broken cog**
Before the club he had done "my turn in the barrel" with network marketing companies and weekend real estate seminars. Joint ventures with his real estate mentor (recorded calls sold as CDs on eBay, a nationwide wholesaling network) all fizzled, and he takes the blame for every one.
Use it for: owning a failure to implement; the student as the weak link.
Told in: Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018); 1 telling.
Quote: "it was always my fault that they didn't go anywhere. I was the broken cog."

**The mentor on the bankruptcy schedule**
His bankruptcy lawyer kept asking for more creditors, so he listed his ex-wife and his real estate mentor, the mentor for $35,000 in "unpaid consulting fees" nobody could ever collect. Years later, doing well, he bought the mentor a massage chair of about $3,000.
Use it for: gratitude as the currency of mentorship (told as a running joke).
Told in: Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018); 1 telling.
Quote: "I actually listed him as a creditor on my bankruptcy to the tune of $35,000 in unpaid consulting fees"

**Bankruptcy, his second-best decision**
He ranks joining the Navy instead of college first, filing Chapter 7 second, and falling into tax resolution third. His regret is filing a year too late, after stopping payments in early 2007.
Use it for: taking the stigma off bankruptcy; discomfort forcing a better career.
Told in: Discomfort Atlanta (2015), Lien Update (2018), Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018); 3 tellings.
Quote: "filing chapter seven bankruptcy was the second best decision i ever made in my life"

**FICO can shove it**
After the bankruptcy he swore off credit entirely, even letting a $4,000 gap-insurance shortfall on a wrecked truck be written off. Then he reversed course: $50-60 a month for FICO's three-bureau monitoring, the myFICO forums, an entry-level Capital One card, and applications "left and right."
Use it for: practical credit rebuilding, and his habit of going all the way into any subject.
Told in: Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018), Prospecting Online (undated); 2 tellings.
Quote: "I will never utilize the credit system. FICO can shove it."
Check: Prospecting Online says the forum advice raised his scores enough to buy a home soon after the 2008 bankruptcy; the 2018 interview dates his first purchase to 2015.

## Navy and early work

**Nuclear Navy and Chernobyl**
He spent his first six years of adulthood as a Navy nuclear operator, where nobody turns a valve or opens a breaker without a reviewed checklist. He retells Chernobyl as a shift crew deliberately overriding three written procedures to run a test because they were bored.
Use it for: verbatim compliance with written procedures; checklists for anything repeated, delegated, or commonly botched, especially getting paid.
Told in: Leadership webinar (c. 2013), TPS Miami (2014), Strategies (2015), Orlando (2016), Marketing Plan Pt 4 (2017), One-Hour Q&A (2019); 6 tellings.
Quote: "You don't turn a single valve anywhere. You don't flip a switch without referring to a checklist."
Check: role given as "nuclear power technician" (Strategies 2015), "nuclear power plant operator" (TPS Miami 2014; Orlando 2016), and "nuclear power operator aboard a U.S. Navy aircraft carrier" (One-Hour Q&A 2019).

**The Navy tax center**
Parked on shore duty in his last months before discharge in 2001, he built a military tax assistance center in a World War II barracks from surplus parts and a crew of sailors on their way out. In the fullest telling he networked the building, built 15 computers, taught himself TaxWise in three days, trained 15 sailors in two, and personally processed 5,000 rejects.
Use it for: where his tax footing came from, told against himself.
Told in: Blueprint (2015), Discomfort Atlanta (2015), 1120S (2015), Marketing 101 (c. 2017), Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018), Top Strategies (2020); 6 tellings.
Quote: "I personally processed 5,000 rejects. That tells you how bad I trained everybody on doing the tax returns."
Check:
- Scale: "the largest military tax assistance center in the entire US military" (Marketing 101); "largest in the Department of Defense" (Blueprint 2015); 40,000 returns (Discomfort Atlanta 2015); the 2018 interview host shortens it to about three months of sailors' returns.
- Place and time: Norfolk, Virginia, last three months (Discomfort Atlanta 2015); "last few months" (Marketing 101).

**The degree he never used**
His first wife made finishing his degree a condition of accepting his proposal. Three years of college in the Navy, then three more years to finish the last one, in nuclear engineering, in a state with no nuclear plants.
Use it for: self-deprecation; he should have studied accounting.
Told in: Discomfort Atlanta (2015); 1 telling.
Quote: "It took me three years to finish my last year of college."

**Gambling sites on eBay**
In the late 1990s, still in the Navy, he and a shipmate built turnkey affiliate gambling websites and sold them on eBay for about $500 each, out-earning his military pay. Nervous buyers wanted a phone call first, so it was really a telephone sale.
Use it for: his first sales job.
Told in: Sales Conversions (undated); 1 telling.
Quote: "I was making more money doing that than I was from the military."

**Selling holes in the ground**
His first civilian job, in 2001, was selling rural well drilling in Colorado: pulling state well permit applications, hunting phone numbers, reading USGS maps and well-depth data, walking the land with a stick and a crank tape measure. He closed by asking for "authorization" to schedule the rig, and made most of his money on the filtration upsell by selling Denver transplants "fresh mountain water" rather than hydrogeology.
Use it for: a well permit is a tax lien; ask for authorization (sign the 2848) rather than shoving a contract across the desk; sell the outcome, not the spec.
Told in: Discomfort Atlanta (2015), Marketing Plan Pt 4 (2017), Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018), One-Hour Q&A (2019), Sales Training (2020), Sales Conversions (undated); 6 tellings.
Quote: "That was my first job in the civilian world, was selling holes in the ground."
Check: a $5,000-6,000 hole at an estimated 280-320 feet (Marketing Plan Pt 4 2017) versus a $7,000, 200-foot cased hole (Sales Conversions, undated).

**Ad sales and the book that fixed his selling**
He sold water treatment, websites, and advertising for a free monthly Fort Collins paper (where he once pitched the founder of his investor club), and hated the classic closes those jobs taught. In his twenties he found a late-1980s book called Needs Analysis Selling, author forgotten, and calls it a monumental discovery.
Use it for: he knows old-school sales from the inside, and consultative selling (diagnose, then prescribe) is the model for licensed professionals.
Told in: Bankrupt-to-$1M interview (2018), One-Hour Q&A (2019), Sales Training (2020), 941 Plan (2020), Sales Conversions (undated); 5 tellings.
Quote: "I've sold holes in the ground. I've sold water treatment. I've sold websites. I've sold newspaper advertising."

## Building the practice

**Employee number six**
A friend fresh out of a Kaplan paralegal program got him an interview at a small Denver resolution firm whose business she couldn't explain beyond "people that owe money to the government." It took minutes to clear a chair for the interview; files were piled everywhere, 433s sat in the wrong folders, and the only lead source was a boiler room of telemarketers looking up lien phone numbers by hand. He took the job at $10 an hour with an hour's commute each way; in the 2016 telling there were drugs on the sales floor, and it "took a guy smoking meth in the men's room" before anyone was fired.
Use it for: an industry so backward that any improvement looks revolutionary.
Told in: Leadership webinar (c. 2013), TPS Miami (2014), Strategies (2015), Blueprint (2015), Discomfort Atlanta (2015), Orlando (2016), Marketing 101 (c. 2017), Getting to One (2018), Top Strategies (2020); 9 tellings.
Quote: "I went from the leading edge to, you know, an industry that, you know, the rotary phone was a step forward."
Check:
- Start: "stumbled into" it in January 2008 (Top Strategies 2020); the 2018 interview has him working there before the April 2008 foreclosure.
- Months as an admin before moving into marketing: five (Strategies 2015); six (Blueprint 2015; Top Strategies 2020); "four or five months in" he was already starting a prep division (Discomfort Atlanta 2015).

**$65K to $230K a month**
He added tracking, a CRM, direct mail, internet marketing, and long-term follow-up over owners who insisted tax debtors wouldn't respond, and the firm's monthly revenue more than tripled in under a year and a half. It is the central credential in nearly every bio he gives.
Use it for: systems, tracking, and follow-up beat "our business is different."
Told in: Leadership webinar (c. 2013), TPS Miami (2014), Strategies (2015), Blueprint (2015), Discomfort Atlanta (2015), Orlando (2016), Marketing Plan Pt 4 (2017), Marketing 101 (c. 2017), Getting to One (2018), San Diego (2018), One-Hour Q&A (2019), Referrals Office Hours (2020), Top Strategies (2020); 13 tellings.
Quote: "353% monthly revenue growth in 17 months."
Check:
- Growth: $65,000 to $230,000 a month in 17 months or under 18 (Leadership c. 2013; TPS Miami 2014; Strategies 2015; Blueprint 2015; Orlando 2016; Marketing 101; Top Strategies 2020); $65,000 to $285,000 a month in 17 months (Discomfort Atlanta 2015); half a million to $2.5 million a year (Marketing Plan Pt 4 2017); $750,000 a year with six employees to $3.3 million with 40 (Getting to One 2018); $750,000 to over $3 million (Top Strategies 2020); under $1 million to over $3 million (One-Hour Q&A 2019); $65,000 to "a quarter million" a month (San Diego 2018).
- The percentage: $230K over $65K is about 3.5 times, a 254% increase; "353%" may be the ratio rather than the growth.
- Dates: June 2008 to November 2009 (TPS Miami 2014; Top Strategies 2020); changes began July 2008 (Marketing 101; Orlando 2016).
- Afterward: held at $3-3.2 million a year (Marketing 101); about $3 million or $3.25 million a year, or $250,000-280,000 a month (Orlando 2016 and Leadership c. 2013); $300,000-400,000 months with nearly 40 staff (TPS Miami 2014); over $4 million with about 45 staff by 2014 (Discomfort Atlanta 2015).
- Testing spend: "a couple hundred thousand dollars" (Strategies 2015) versus "nearly $500,000" of the employer's money (Marketing 101).

**The phone-lookup program**
The telemarketers looked up every lien's phone number by hand, so he put the liens in a spreadsheet and spent about three days writing a program to do it.
Use it for: one small tool beats more effort.
Told in: Discomfort Atlanta (2015), Orlando (2016); 2 tellings.
Quote: "that one step tripled our daily telemarketing capacity and it took me about three days to write that little piece of software"
Check: tripled daily calling capacity and lifted revenue $30,000 the next month (Discomfort Atlanta 2015) versus cut sales man-hours almost in half (Orlando 2016).

**The CRM nobody sold**
No tax resolution CRM existed in 2008, so in June 2008 he programmed his own on his own time (later TaxCRM), handling lead tracking and the engagement letter, 2848, and 8821. The owners told him internet and direct mail would never work on tax debtors.
Use it for: building the tool you need; the owners' quote as the industry's blind spot.
Told in: TPS Miami (2014), Blueprint (2015), Orlando (2016); 3 tellings.
Quote: "a quote from one of the owners was people on the internet aren't looking to spend money"

**Back returns by hand**
He talked the firm into bringing tax prep in-house (it had been sending about $10,000 a month to the H&R Block three doors down) and then spent a year doing old-year returns on paper forms downloaded from the web, because the owners wouldn't buy software. He later learned that a cheap ATX license included ten prior years.
Use it for: why he swore off prep when he went solo; also "I didn't know any better."
Told in: Discomfort Atlanta (2015), 12-Step live (2018), First Hire (undated); 3 tellings.
Quote: "Pen and paper tax returns. In 2008. I was doing 1998 through 2007 returns on paper. Because I didn't know any better."
Check: years done by hand were 1998-2007 (12-Step live 2018) versus 2001-2003 (First Hire); ATX price $500 (12-Step live) and learned of it in 2010 (First Hire); the software budget the firm refused was $5,000 (First Hire).

**Firing the daycare centers**
His first analysis of good and bad clients at the Denver firm showed daycare centers stuck between late state child-care reimbursements and adult-to-child ratio laws, so they could neither pay nor cut payroll. The firm stopped taking them, or demanded certified funds up front, and its receivables problem largely disappeared; in one telling it also dropped three states whose clients stiffed it.
Use it for: Pareto analysis of who pays; turning away a whole category can make you more money.
Told in: Blueprint (2015), Orlando (2016), Marketing 101 (c. 2017), Quoting Fees (2017), Screening Masterclass (2018), One-Hour Q&A (2019); 6 tellings.
Quote: "So we just chose not to represent them anymore. That resolved over half of our client payment problems."
Check:
- Effect: resolved over half of payment problems (Marketing 101); receivables cut 80-90% overnight (One-Hour Q&A 2019); 70-80% of problem clients were child care providers and collections "dropped like a rock" (Quoting Fees 2017).
- Timing: his "first big observation" (One-Hour Q&A 2019) versus about a year into the industry (Blueprint 2015).
- Contrary evidence: in the Bookkeeping webinar (2010) he says daycare centers are "such a large part of my client base"; in Fast Start Lead Gen (2013) the policy is half up front, not refusal.

**$500 times forever**
The firm's salespeople sold $7,000 fees at $500 a month, clients stopped paying, and he made it stop. In his own practice he required at least $1,000 down, pushed for half, took active-levy clients only with $500 extra in certified funds, and stopped taking checks in 2012.
Use it for: you work with professional debtors; don't become another creditor.
Told in: Blueprint (2015), Orlando (2016), Quoting Fees (2017); 3 tellings.
Quote: "The $500 times forever had to stop. And I'm like, no, we need some money up front."

**The double bird**
After a fight over equity, a partnership that never came, and pay for intellectual property he built on his own time, he walked out of the Denver firm on October 24, 2010, keeping his IP. In the live 2016 version he flipped his boss a double bird, about-faced, and decided halfway down the hall it was uncalled for.
Use it for: going solo without hesitating; a candid, self-mocking moment.
Told in: Leadership webinar (c. 2013), TPS Miami (2014), Blueprint (2015), Orlando (2016); 4 tellings.
Quote: "I flipped the double bird did an about face walked out the door"
Check:
- Reason: equity and about $30,000 owed (Orlando 2016); a partnership that never materialized and an IP compensation dispute (TPS Miami 2014); the firm drifting into unethical sales practices (Blueprint 2015); "we agreed that my tenure was over" (Leadership c. 2013).
- Date: October 24, 2010 (most tellings) versus October 26, 2010 (Marketing 101).
- Next step: launched taxlienshq.net and a private practice the very next day (TPS Miami 2014) versus formed a Colorado LLC two days later (Orlando 2016).

**Going solo on $9,000**
He put every dollar into direct mail, Google pay-per-click, and SEO, cold-called tax liens for hours a day because he had no budget for anything else, and plowed his first fee, a $500 levy release, straight back into ads. By August 2011 he had a full caseload and staff.
Use it for: when you're broke, marketing costs time instead of money; the results were repeatable, not a fluke.
Told in: Leadership webinar (c. 2013), TPS Miami (2014), Strategies (2015), Blueprint (2015), Discomfort Atlanta (2015), 1120S (2015), Orlando (2016), Marketing Plan Pt 4 (2017), Marketing 101 (c. 2017), Lien Update (2018), Getting to One (2018), One-Hour Q&A (2019), Top Strategies (2020); 13 tellings.
Quote: "I spent six to eight hours a day cold calling tax liens because I didn't have a marketing budget. Okay. So I smiled and dialed, smiled and dialed. It sucked."
Check:
- Savings: just under $10,000 (Strategies 2015); about $9,000 (Marketing 101; One-Hour Q&A 2019; Top Strategies 2020); $9,000-10,000 (Blueprint 2015); about $10,000 (Orlando 2016); $11,000-12,000 (TPS Miami 2014).
- Hours: 14-hour days, six or seven on the phone (Strategies 2015); 12-14 hours, six to eight on the phone (Marketing 101); 10-14 hours, seven days a week, six to eight on the phone (Orlando 2016); 12-14 hours with four to six of telemarketing (TPS Miami 2014); 100-150 calls a day for three or four months (One-Hour Q&A 2019).
- Result by August 2011: 38-39 clients and $35,000-38,000 a month (Strategies 2015); $38,000 billed with 38 active cases (Marketing 101); 38 cases and $35,000 (Blueprint 2015); 37-38 cases and $35,000 (TPS Miami 2014); 40-plus cases and just over $30,000, about $31,800 (Leadership c. 2013); $35,000 (Orlando 2016); just under $40,000 with four staff (Discomfort Atlanta 2015); "more than half a million a year in less than a year" (Referrals Office Hours 2020).
- Staff then: first sales support hire in March or April 2011 (One-Hour Q&A 2019); three part-time remote helpers (Blueprint 2015); one salesperson and a part-time paralegal (TPS Miami 2014); four (Discomfort Atlanta 2015; Outsourcing 2018).

**Sleeping in the Provo office**
In January 2011 he moved to Provo, Utah ("for a girl, but that's a totally unrelated story"), rented an office, and slept in it for the first couple of months so every dollar went to the business. He introduces it as something he had never told publicly.
Use it for: extreme measures, offered as confession, not prescription.
Told in: Blueprint (2015); 1 telling.
Quote: "here's something that I've never, ever, ever publicly told people."
Check: the other tellings place the early solo practice in Colorado (Colorado LLC in Orlando 2016; Fort Collins mailbox in 12-Step live 2018).

**The eight that wouldn't die**
Back from Japan in late 2011, he gave uncooperative clients hard deadlines, pushed revenue officers and their group managers, let both salespeople go, and cut the practice to eight cases. He has been a one-man band since laying off his last assistant in March 2012, and for years held income well under six figures on purpose so he could work five or six hours a week.
Use it for: shrinking a practice deliberately to buy freedom; owning the choice without apology.
Told in: Discomfort Atlanta (2015); 1 telling.
Quote: "They were like the eight that wouldn't die, but they were still paying me on time."

**Four slots and the 72-hour blitzkrieg**
In 2012-13 he kept a fixed number of active resolution cases, and when a slot opened he ran a set campaign: postcards to a filtered 941 lien list, then a voice broadcast a day or two after they landed. Overseas, one case could carry him two or three months, so he could be very picky.
Use it for: a capacity rule plus a repeatable fill-the-slot campaign; selectivity depends on deal flow.
Told in: Fire Worst Clients (2013), Dark Magic (2013), Screening Masterclass (2018), Referrals Office Hours (2020); 4 tellings.
Quote: "I was charging enough money where one case at a time could tide me over for two or three months"
Check: case cap of four (Dark Magic 2013; Fire Worst Clients 2013); one to four (Screening Masterclass 2018); eight in late 2011 (Discomfort Atlanta 2015); 10-20 a year (Remote Practice 2018; San Diego 2018).

**Firing 40 prep clients, then snorkeling in Nassau**
After a tax season in Vancouver, Washington convinced him he hated prep, he told all but three or four of about 40 ongoing prep clients to find someone else in fall 2013. He gave the talk from a condo 200 feet from Cable Beach in Nassau, after a morning of snorkeling.
Use it for: offer only the services you enjoy; build a boutique practice around the life.
Told in: Fire Worst Clients (2013); 1 telling.
Quote: "I spent most of the morning snorkeling and uh to be honest once once we we wrap up uh our call here i'm probably going to go back out to the water."
Check: 1120S (2015) and Top Strategies (2020) say he has worked only three tax seasons in his life (Navy 2001, Jackson Hewitt 2008, and one more); this 2013 Vancouver season and the 2015 Seattle-area season would make at least four.

**Apply to be my client**
His website button said "request a case review," and for stretches he refused to schedule any call until a prospect returned the worksheet, a budget, a net worth sheet, a 2848, an engagement letter, and a card authorization. Emailed packets in one period he deleted unread and blamed the fax machine. Prospects decided he must be in demand, and the few who complied closed almost every time.
Use it for: make prospects jump through hoops; closing rate is a function of the filter; positioning through scarcity.
Told in: Marketing Plan Pt 4 (2017), Quoting Fees (2017), 12-Step live (2018), San Diego (2018), Screening Masterclass (2018), Sales Training (2020), 941 Plan (2020); 7 tellings.

## Recurring analogies

- **Cardiac surgeon versus family physician**, same license, very different pay → specialists earn more and get more referrals (at least six talks).
- **Nuclear reactor checklists and Chernobyl** → every repeated or delegated task needs a written checklist.
- **A tax form is a checklist; the IRM is the IRS's checklists** → checklist thinking is already the profession's language.
- **A doctor who diagnoses and prescribes; the process handout as a prescription pad; the elective-procedure close ("We can squeeze you in Tuesday at three")** → consultative selling from authority.
- **Selling from the right side of the desk; physicians don't cold-call; invited guest, not annoying pest** (James Orr) → positioning so prospects come to you.
- **Well permit = tax lien; drilling estimate = fee quote; expired listings = people burned by fly-by-night firms** → translating tactics across industries.
- **Boiler Room, Wolf of Wall Street, Glengarry Glen Ross, IRS impersonator scam calls** → how the national resolution firms sell.
- **Spin-off firms as a cancer or a spider web** → how bad practices spread through an incestuous industry.
- **The rotary phone was a step forward** → how backward the industry was in 2008.
- **Big fish in a small pond; a pond the big firms weren't fishing in; boutique is another word for niched** → niching against national competitors.
- **USA Today versus Trucker News; shotgun versus precision shooting (as a former competitive shooter)** → targeted media is cheaper and better.
- **A truck driver with a gun rack and deer trophies doesn't listen to NPR** → market, message, and media alignment.
- **The avalanche or tsunami of calls after a lien filing; marketing in a vacuum; showing up on a shiny stallion for the rebounds** → why to market to aged liens.
- **New homebuyers buy furniture; lien filers need representation** → the lien list as the obvious list.
- **Dating: you don't propose at the bar; cross-sell is marriage** → each step's goal is the next step, not the sale.
- **A series of funnels; a leaky funnel** → lead generation, nurture, and follow-up as stacked stages with loss at each.
- **Lipstick and a dress (or makeup and high heels) on a free consultation** → renaming free offers to raise perceived value.
- **Free-dinner seminars and the "plate lickers"** → one-to-many selling works despite freeloaders.
- **Consultations taking food off your kid's table; time vampires; you are not a Walmart greeter** → why to charge for or filter consultations.
- **A college application fee; a teacher grading homework at home** → charging prospects to apply, and cherry-picking from intake forms.
- **We don't work in an ER, ride an ambulance, or fight fires; the garage on fire at 3 a.m. is a real emergency** → nothing a tax pro does is an emergency.
- **The cell phone as a leash; a Jiffy Lube owner can't work the bays from Greece** → always-reachable professionals can't have a lifestyle practice.
- **Answering your own phone is a $10-an-hour job** → delegate if you want to bill $500 an hour.
- **You are not Coca-Cola (or Pepsi, Ford, GM)** → small firms do direct response, not image advertising.
- **A license from the government to print money; a golden ticket** → licensed practitioners who only do prep waste the license.
- **The business burns down but you keep the list on a thumb drive** → the house list is the most valuable asset.
- **An investment policy statement; a business plan for your personal life** → write the lifestyle rules your practice must serve.
- **$50,000 a year of part-time work equals a $1 million portfolio** → semi-retirement for the under-saved.
- **Pareto's pea pods; Parkinson's pre-vacation burst** → 80/20 client analysis and time blocking.
- **A Leatherman, not a hammer** → the first hire must be a generalist.
- **Contractors are tools like your copier lease** → judge outsourcing on cost and profit, not feelings.
- **A kid forced to fold laundry and eat Brussels sprouts** → running a practice outside your zone of genius.
- **Snowmelt dribbles becoming the Colorado River** → many small marketing feeders compounding.
- **The financial analysis is a mortgage application** → explaining Forms 433-A, B, and F to a prospect.
- **The IRS dragon is sleeping** → working cases and marketing while collections is paused (2020).
- **A smartphone is a video studio in your pocket** → no excuse for skipping video.
- **Missionaries' pass-along cards** → always give a prospect something to take away.
- **The bar is lying on the ground** → how little competitors do; just step over it.
- **Business is a battlefield** (with an apology) → strategy versus tactics.
- **Internal accountant versus inner 12-year-old** → the split between his rational and emotional money selves.
