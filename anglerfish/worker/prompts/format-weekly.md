# The weekly post

One shape, every week. Three movements, then the close. When the requested
format is `weekly`, ignore the six rotating formats above — two of them survive
here as components, and neither is a post type any more.

Roughly 900-1400 words of body. Longer than the daily format, and long enough
that the diagnostic has room to work.

## 1. The Trap

What the reader is doing wrong, or the structural mistake most practices make.

Cold open on the claim. The first sentence is the contrarian claim, the
compressed statistic, or the provocative observation — no windup, no "in today's
economy," no scene-setting paragraph before the point.

A failure-first opening is the signature move here: admit Jassen made the same
mistake he is about to explain. *"I've fallen into this thinking trap many, many
times."* Credibility through shared failure beats leading with research.

One vivid human scenario, in stacked punchy phrases rather than full sentences.
Oil change plus mandatory overtime. Massage plus soccer season. Specific enough
to recognise, universal enough to land.

Close the movement with the turn: one short standalone line that flips the
reader's assumption.

## 2. The Diagnostic

The internal analysis step, and the load-bearing movement. Most business writing
skips it and jumps straight to advice. Do not skip it.

The reader applies something to their own practice, right now, and arrives at
the diagnosis themselves — diagnostic questions, a measurement to take, or a
number to calculate. Supply no answers to question-style diagnostics; the
questions do the work by surfacing what the reader already knows and has been
avoiding.

Close with a threshold: *"If you said no to more than two of these, you have
your project for the week."*

**One Number lives here.** Where a single metric or threshold carries the
diagnosis, open on the number, say what it means, say how to measure it, and say
what to do depending on where the reader lands.

## 3. The Protocol

What to do about it. Numbered, sequential, literal. Not strategy — a procedure
someone can execute today or hand to an employee.

Write it out in full, in the post body. The PDF is the working copy of what the
reader just read, formatted to be used; it is never the continuation of the
post. A reader on a phone who will never download anything gets the complete
piece.

**Steal This lives here.** Where the protocol is a real tactic from a real
business, name it, attribute it, and reverse-engineer it into replicable parts.
No editorialising about how brilliant it is.

## 4. Go!

Warmer, more personal and more playful than the body. Not a corporate checklist.
Some mix of:

- Direct challenges and gentle goading: *"Mosey along, now."* / *"Dude. Stop
  reading."*
- An offer to engage: *"Post a comment, I'll respond personally."*
- Repetition of the single most important action
- A named technique — give the move a name ("the quit-offer audit", "the cut
  list")
- Tool references where genuinely useful
- The download link, mentioned once, plainly

It should read like a friend who knows you are going to procrastinate on this
and is lovingly calling you out.

## Shape and rhythm

- Title is a reframe, not a topic. Curiosity gap or contrarian flip.
- One idea only. A second idea becomes next week's post.
- Sub-section openers double as headers: the punchy aphorism IS the first
  sentence of the paragraph, not a separate bold line above it.
- Short paragraphs, white space as rhythm, one-sentence paragraphs allowed —
  but prefer paragraphs longer than two sentences where the narrative still
  flows the way a person actually talks.
- Break into the deliverable: drop out of prose straight into the hard
  copy-paste. The abrupt shift is itself a rhythm device.

## The standing risk

The biggest failure mode of this format is the post degrading into an ad for the
download. Full trap, full diagnostic, full protocol in the body. Anything that
renders as an ordered or unordered list belongs in both the post and the PDF.
